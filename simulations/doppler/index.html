Doppler shifts of a rotating planet

A rotating planet broadens its spectral lines: the approaching limb is blueshifted and the receding limb is redshifted. When a dark or bright spot rotates across the disk, it leaves a bump or a dip that drifts through the line profile. This is the principle behind Doppler imaging of directly-imaged planets and brown dwarfs, like the work I did on VHS 1256 b.

What to look for

  • Dark spot → bump, bright spot → dip. A dark spot hides some of the absorbing area at its velocity, so the line gets shallower there. A bright spot adds weight, so the line gets deeper. The faint opposite-sign halo around a bump is the continuum normalisation: when a dark spot hides light, the rest of the line looks relatively deeper.
  • Position sets the shift. A feature at horizontal position x on the disk (in units of the radius) has v = veq sin i · x, so its velocity follows the sinusoidal path of the spot across the disk.
  • North–south ambiguity. At i = 90°, spots at latitudes +φ and −φ give identical signals. Lowering the inclination breaks the symmetry and keeps low-latitude spots from reaching the far limb.
  • Limits of the model. Uniform limb-darkened disk, a spot changes the continuum only (the intrinsic line depth is unchanged), no noise, no differential rotation.
