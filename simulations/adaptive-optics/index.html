Adaptive optics loop

The atmosphere distorts the wavefront of starlight, blurring the image of a star into a flickering halo of speckles. An adaptive optics system measures this distortion many times per second and cancels it with a deformable mirror. Switch the loop on and off, then see what limits the correction: the number of actuators, the speed of the loop, the wind, and the brightness of the guide star.

What to look for

  • Fitting the profile. A Gaussian describes a smooth core, a Moffat adds the broad wings of the seeing halo, and an Airy function is the diffraction pattern of a circular telescope. Choose one function or the sum of two, for example Airy + Moffat for a sharp core inside a halo, and watch the FWHM move between the diffraction limit (1.03 λ/D) and the seeing limit (0.98 λ/r₀).
  • Mirror motion. The faceplate follows the turbulence (dashed) but more smoothly, because the actuators can only reproduce scales larger than their spacing, and a little late, because of the loop delay. With the loop open the mirror stays flat.
  • Loop open vs closed. Without correction the image is a seeing-limited cloud of speckles about λ / r₀ wide. With the loop closed, light collapses into a sharp core with Airy rings, and the Strehl ratio rises.
  • Number of actuators. The mirror only corrects spatial scales larger than twice the actuator pitch, so it controls the image out to Nact / 2 · λ/D (the square). Outside it, the halo stays. The leftover is the fitting error, about 0.22 (d / r₀)5/3 rad² for this ideal mirror (about 0.28 for a real one).
  • Loop speed and wind. The mirror always corrects the turbulence of a few milliseconds ago. A slower loop, a longer delay or a stronger wind increases this temporal error and leaves a halo stretched along the wind direction.
  • Gain and noise. A faint guide star gives few photons per subaperture, so the measurement is noisy. Lowering the gain filters the noise but slows the correction. A high gain with a long delay makes the loop unstable.
  • Limits of the model. The PSF has two pixels per λ/D, so FWHM values near the diffraction limit are only good to a few per cent. With the loop open, a few seconds of long exposure hold few independent samples of the large-scale tilt, so the measured FWHM changes by about ±20 % from one run to the next (use Restart) and, averaged over runs, lies within about 10 % of the von Kármán estimate. Single frozen turbulent layer moving at constant speed, on a periodic screen four pupil diameters wide completed with subharmonics for the large scales. An ideal wavefront sensor and mirror (no aliasing, no non-linearity), no chromatic effects, and a simplified photon-noise estimate.
