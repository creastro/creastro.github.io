Telescope point spread function

A telescope cannot focus the light of a star to a perfect point. Light is a wave, so every aperture blurs the star into a pattern of rings and spikes called the point spread function (PSF). This simulation computes that pattern as the Fourier transform of the aperture. Change the size of the mirror, the central obstruction, the spider vanes and the aberrations, or build the mirror from hexagonal segments like Keck and JWST, and watch how the image of the star responds.

What to look for

  • Resolution. A bigger mirror or a shorter wavelength makes the star image smaller. The first dark ring sits at 1.22 λ/D, so doubling the diameter halves it. The field of view is fixed in arcseconds, so you see the core shrink.
  • Airy rings. A clear circular mirror gives a bright core and rings that fade outward; the first bright ring holds 1.75 % of the peak. Raise the dynamic range to 6 or 8 decades to follow the faint outer rings.
  • Central obstruction. The secondary mirror moves light from the core into the rings. The open area falls as 1 − ε² and the peak as (1 − ε²)², while the first rings get brighter.
  • Spider vanes. Each vane makes a spike perpendicular to it. An even number N of vanes gives N spikes and an odd number gives 2N. Thinner vanes give fainter spikes that reach further out.
  • Aberrations. Defocus and spherical aberration fill in the dark rings, astigmatism stretches the core in one direction, and coma gives a comet-shaped tail. For a small error σ (in waves) the Strehl ratio falls as exp[−(2πσ)²], so 0.075 wave RMS is already enough to drop to 0.8.
  • Segmented mirror. A honeycomb makes six diffraction spikes from the hexagonal segments and the hexagonal outline of the array; try the JWST-like preset. Random piston and tip/tilt errors break the phasing: the sharp core fades into a speckle halo and the Strehl ratio falls as exp[−(2πσp)²]. Press New random draw to see another set of errors with the same RMS.
  • Bandwidth. The rings of different wavelengths have different sizes, so a wide band blurs the rings and fills the dark ones. A geometric error is a fixed length, so in waves it gets smaller at longer wavelengths.
  • Limits of the model. Far-field (Fraunhofer) image of an on-axis point source, with no atmosphere (see the adaptive optics page for turbulence), no detector sampling and no noise. The grid has 4 samples per λ/D and covers ±64 λ/D; light beyond it wraps around, which is negligible for clean apertures. Interpolation lifts the deepest dark rings slightly above theory. The wavelength colour map only illustrates hue and does not model a source spectrum or the response of the eye. Segment edges are sampled 8 × 8 within a pixel, so the light scattered by gaps far thinner than a pixel is only approximate. Segment errors are piston and tip/tilt only, with no curvature errors or edge roll-off.
