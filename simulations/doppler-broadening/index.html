Doppler Effect & Rotational Line Broadening

Understanding stellar motion through astronomical spectroscopy. A spectral line moves when its source moves along our line of sight, and it widens when the source spins, because one half of the disk approaches while the other recedes. A spectrograph adds the light of the whole disk, so the line it records is the sum of all these shifts.

Sign convention: positive radial velocity = receding = redshift.

What to look for

  • The sign of vr sets the colour. Positive radial velocity is a source moving away: its light is stretched to longer wavelengths (z > 0, redshift). A negative one gives a blueshift, and zero gives no shift at all.
  • Slow or fast. For |vr| ≪ c the two formulas agree to β²/2. At 30 km/s that is 5 × 10⁻⁹ in z, far below anything visible. Switch to the 0.9 c range to see them part: at β = 0.5 the relativistic z is 0.73 instead of 0.5.
  • One disk, many shifts. Each strip of the disk has its own shift, vlos = v sin i · x, and the spectrograph adds them all. The edge of the line is at ±Δλmax = ±λ v sin i / c, where the limbs of the star are. A single surface element has a shift but no broadening.
  • Inclination. Slide i towards 0° with veq fixed: v sin i shrinks to zero and the line returns to its unbroadened shape. At i = 90° the broadening is largest.
  • Limb darkening makes the dim limbs count less, so the profile gets a more pointed centre and shorter wings than the half-ellipse of ε = 0.
  • Radial speed is not rotation speed. In C changing vr moves the line without changing its width, and changing v sin i changes the width without moving the centroid.
  • Limits of the model. Rigid rotation, linear limb darkening, one Gaussian intrinsic line (no pressure or Zeeman effects, no macroturbulence, no instrument profile), no differential rotation, no noise. The broadening is evaluated in the observer's frame by scaling the width with λobs.
